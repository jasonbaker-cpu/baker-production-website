What to say

Learn the shape, say it your way. Never apologise for the gap — no "sorry I dropped off", no "that's on me". Lead with what you've got on, not why you went quiet.

Before you dial

Prep the images first. Find one of their current listings, pick the two worst photos, clean them up, have them sitting in a draft. Don't dial until those exist — the whole call rests on them.

Target is dials, not wins. Voicemail counts. A no counts. Five dials, fifteen minutes, stop when the bar fills even if every one says no.

Warmest name first, always. Someone who's booked you ten times isn't a cold call, and once you've talked to one human the next four are easy.

The nerves go about twenty seconds into the call, not before it. More preparing is just more avoiding — memorise two lines and dial.

Timing: Monday and Tuesday mornings, or 3–4pm Tuesday to Thursday. Avoid the middle of Wednesday and Thursday — they're prepping for the weekend. Never Saturday, never during a home open.

The one rule that covers everything: pause after every question mark, and never answer your own question.

Making the call go well

Talk slower than feels right. Nerves double your speed and speed is the single thing that makes you sound like a telemarketer. Deliberately slow — it will feel odd and sound normal.

Stand up and smile. Not a wellness tip — posture and face change the sound of your voice and they can hear it.

Say their name once, early. Not three times, that's salesman.

Count three seconds after your question. The silence is theirs. Whoever fills it first loses.

Have the photos actually ready to send before you dial. Fumbling for them afterwards is how "within the hour" becomes tomorrow.

Log it in HQ the second you hang up — outcome, note, callback date. Ninety seconds later you'll have forgotten what he said.

You cannot control whether they book. You control the speed, the pause, and whether the photos land. Judge yourself on those three and nothing else.

Cold agent — never worked with them

Across 90,380 recorded cold calls: a warm human opener lifts booking rate 3.4×, saying "the reason I'm calling" lifts it 2.1×, and asking permission up front ("got a minute?", "bad time?") performs 40% worse than saying nothing at all. Permission before the reason kills the call — permission after it helps.

"Hi, is that [name]? It's Jason, from Baker Production — (pause) we do real estate photography here in Perth."

▮ Wait for "yeah, speaking." They have to answer this one. "Is that [name]?" earns a yes before you've asked for anything, and it means you're not pitching the wrong person. The (pause) after your own name is half a beat — a breath, not a wait.

"I saw you just listed [address] — I cleaned up two of the photos, took [specific thing] out of the [room] shot."

Name what you actually removed, from the actual photo. The bins, the benchtop clutter, the washing basket, the car in the driveway. This one detail is the whole reason the call works — look at the picture before you dial.

"It's completely free, no catch — just wanted to send them over."

"Did you want me to send them through?"

▮▮▮ THREE FULL SECONDS. Say nothing. This is where the call is won or lost. Fill it and you turn a gift into a pitch.

Two optional swaps, take them or leave them. "No charge" instead of "completely free, no catch" — saying "no catch" can plant the idea there is one. And "Want me to send them through?" instead of "Did you want" — present tense is a bit stronger. Neither matters more than saying it like you mean it.

"Is [first]@[agency].com.au the right one?"

▮ Short — they just confirm. A tick, not a request, and it proves you did homework. Have the address already off the agency site. If he says "actually send it to admin@", that's gold — that's who books the jobs.

"Are you busy at the moment, or has it gone quiet?"

▮▮▮▮ THE LONG ONE. Could be thirty seconds of him talking — don't interrupt, don't fill, don't move him on. Everything useful you learn comes out here. Peer-to-peer, not qualifying.

"If you like how they come out, try me on one — every shoot comes back like that."

▮▮ Two to three seconds. No softeners — no "no pressure", no "obviously up to you". A trial, not a booking; that's what converts a stranger. This is the only place decluttering gets mentioned on a cold call — after the demo, never before it.

"I'll get those over within the hour — I'll give you a call next week once you've had a look."

Give the callback a reason or it sounds like a sales cadence. Better still, use what they just told you — if he said "two coming next month", say "I'll check in when those come up." That's not a cadence, that's paying attention.

If the call was flat and they clearly wanted off the phone, don't announce a callback at all — just send the photos and set the reminder in HQ quietly. And if you do say it, do it. Otherwise you're the bloke who said he'd ring.

The three pauses that matter: after "send them through", after "busy or quiet", and after "try me on one". Everything else is normal conversation rhythm. Rule for all of them — pause after every question mark, and never answer your own question.

The win is the email and a date, not a booking. If you're sorting newest-first they've already paid someone to shoot that one — you're buying queue position for the next.

Cold call — how it goes right

You: Hi, is that Dave?

Dave: Yeah, speaking.

You: It's Jason, from Baker Production — we do real estate photography here in Perth. I saw you just listed the Karrinyup one — I cleaned up two of the photos, took the bins out of the driveway shot.

Dave: …Sorry, you've edited my photos?

You: Just the two off realestate.com.au. It's completely free, no catch — just wanted to send them over. Did you want me to send them through?

You: ▮▮▮ (nothing. three seconds.)

Dave: Nah go on then, I'm curious.

You: Is dave@[agency].com.au the right one?

Dave: Yep.

You: Beauty. Are you busy at the moment, or has it gone quiet?

Dave: Bit up and down. Got two coming next month, quiet till then.

You: ▮▮▮▮ (say nothing — let him keep going)

Dave: Vendors are all waiting for spring, that's the problem.

You: Yeah, everyone's saying that. Look — if you like how they come out, try me on one. Every shoot comes back like that.

Dave: Yeah maybe. Send them over and we'll see.

You: No worries. I'll get those over within the hour — and I'll check in when those two come up.

Dave: Yeah righto.

That's a win. No booking — but an email, a real conversation, his pipeline ("two next month") and a dated reason to ring back. Note the last line: he used what Dave told him instead of the generic "next week". That's not a cadence, that's paying attention.

Cold call — a no, handled properly

You: Hi, is that Sarah?

Sarah: Yeah? (flat)

You: It's Jason, from Baker Production — we do real estate photography here in Perth. Saw you just listed the Duncraig one — I cleaned up two of the photos, took the clutter off the benches.

Sarah: We've got a photographer, mate.

You: Course you do, everyone does. Not asking you to swap — when they're booked out, who do you call?

Sarah: Doesn't really happen.

You: Fair enough. It's completely free anyway — did you want me to send them through?

Sarah: …Yeah, alright.

You: Is sarah@[agency].com.au right?

Sarah: Yep. Look, I've gotta run.

You: No worries, I'll send them now. Thanks Sarah.

Twenty-five seconds, no booking, went fine. You got the email and the images will land. That's the job. Note what he didn't do: no callback announced, because she clearly wanted off the phone. Set that reminder in HQ quietly instead.

Cold call — the same one, wrecked

Sarah: We've got a photographer, mate.

You: Yeah no worries, I just — I mean I'm not trying to replace anyone, I just do things a bit differently, like I include the decluttering free and I've got this tool where you can do your own photos and I do about fifty jobs a week so turnaround's really quick, and my Essential's only $299 which is—

Sarah: Yeah I'm right, thanks.

Three traps in ten seconds: filling the silence, stacking offers, and pitching price unasked. All three come from panic after a no. The no is not an emergency. Answer it in one line and ask for the email.

Past client — 2 weeks to a month quiet

"Hey [name], Jason from Baker Production — how've you been? … I was going through my calendar and realised I hadn't shot anything for you since [address]. How'd that one end up going?"

"How've you been" is the single best-performing opener there is — 6.6× — because it signals you know each other. With a past client that's true, so use it. Never use it on a stranger.

Then shut up and listen. That's the whole call. Don't fill the pause.

"I'm booking spring dates now — what've you got coming up? Even if it's not locked in I'll pencil you in."

A fortnight isn't a problem, it's a gap. Don't go digging for one — asking "was something wrong?" invents a complaint that wasn't there.

Long gap — 4 to 6 months

"Hey [name], Jason from Baker Production — shot [address] for you back in [month]. How've you been?"

"I'm getting my spring calendar sorted and you were on my list. What have you got coming up?"

No explaining where you've been. No "been flat out" — it reads as an excuse and they never asked.

Name one real detail from that property — the pool, the view, the staircase. Proves it isn't a mass call.

"Send me your next one and I'll do the walkthrough video on me, so you can see how they're coming out now."

Book the follow-up before you hang up. A long gap closes on the second or third call, not the first.

Same agency as an existing client

"Hi [name], Jason from Baker Production — how're you going? … Reason I'm calling, I do the photography for [colleague] in your office."

Not "got a minute?" — that's the 40%-worse opener. Give the reason first, every time.

"I've been shooting their listings for months so I already know your office's style — logo on the web images, your floorplan format, clean high-res. Nothing to set up."

"Have a look at what I did for [colleague] at [address] — that'll tell you more than I can. What's next in your pipeline?"

Never say the colleague recommended you unless they did. "I shoot for them" is true and does the same job.

When they push back

Brush-offs — they're rejecting the call, not you.

"Send me an email."
Don't take it gratefully and hang up, that's how it dies. "Will do. Just so I send the right thing — mostly units or houses? … And I'll give you a bell next week."

"Not interested."
"No worries. Can I send the two photos anyway? They're already sitting there." Costs them nothing to accept, and the images do the work you couldn't.

Flat energy, "uh huh", nothing coming back
Not an objection — a signal. Cut to the exit: "I'll just send the two through, have a look when you get a sec." Leave. Don't push a dead call.

Real walls — don't fight them, go around.

"We've got a photographer."
"Course you do, everyone does. Not asking you to swap — when they're booked out, who do you call?" That question tells you whether a gap exists.

"The agency has a deal / we use [big company]."
Don't argue with an office contract. "Fair enough, that's an office thing. Does it cover your rentals too?" Rentals and off-panel work usually sit outside the deal.

"Take me off your list."
"No worries, sorry to bother you." Hang up, log it Dead, don't send the images.

Doubt about you

"Did you edit my listing photos without asking?"
This will happen and they're not being unreasonable. Give away the power straight away: "Just the two off realestate.com.au — haven't done anything with them other than tidy them up for you. Happy to bin them if you'd rather." Offering to delete ends it on the spot.

"How much?"
Often a filter, not interest. Flat, then hand it back: "$299 Essential — photos and floorplan. Most go Premium at $375 with the drone. What sort of stock are you listing?"

"How old are you?"
No defence, no over-claiming: "Twenty. Been shooting property a few years — we do about fifty jobs a week across Perth." Your volume beats most studios' credentials.

"Who is this? What's this about?"
Don't re-pitch. Compress: "Jason, photographer in Perth — cleaned up two of your [street] photos, want them?"

Gatekeeper — "he's in a meeting"
"No worries, when's he usually about? … I'll try then." Never leave the pitch with reception.

Awkward pause
Let it sit. Whoever speaks first usually loses.

Voicemail + follow-up text

"Hi [name], Jason from Baker Production — 0466 789 522. Did some work on a couple of your [street] photos, give me a buzz when you get a sec. 0466 789 522."

Curiosity, not a pitch. A message they have to think about doesn't get returned. Under 15 seconds, number twice, hang up.

Text: "Hi [name], Jason from Baker Production — just left you a voicemail. Cleaned up two of your [street] photos, no charge. Want me to send them through?"

The text is what actually gets replied to.

The email — send within the hour

The call is worthless without this. Same day isn't good enough; inside the hour is the proof of what you're like to work with.

"Here are those two cleaned up. If you want to run any others — old listings, rentals, anything sitting there — I've put a few credits on an account for you: [link]. Anything I shoot, the decluttering's included."

This is where Studio gets named — never on the call. By now they've seen the result, so the name explains how rather than pitching. It's also your second touch without another phone call.

On the job — first shoot with a new agent

A first-timer becomes an account or a one-off, and it's decided on the day — not by the photos.

Arriving: "Just to confirm — [package], that right? … Anyone home? Anything I should avoid? … And what's the hero of this place, what's the thing that's going to sell it?"

That last question is the whole game. Better photos, and it says you're thinking about selling the house, not just taking pictures. Almost nobody asks it.

Messy room: "Want me to shoot this as is and strip the clutter out digitally after? It's included."

Leaving — three lines, this order:

"You'll have these by [specific time]."

"If anything's not right, reply to the delivery email — I'll fix it same day, no charge."

"Have a look, and if they're what you're after — what else is in the pipeline?"

The revisions line is what converts first-timers. Their fear isn't price, it's being stuck with photos they don't like and having to argue about it.

If they came in on an intro rate, say the real number on the day, while they're happy and you're standing there. Otherwise job two never gets quoted.

On the job — any agent, as you pack up

Best sales moment of your week. Full attention, they've just watched you work, nothing's gone wrong yet.

"What else have you got coming up? I'll pencil you in now."

"Pencil you in now" moves it from a thought to your calendar while you're standing there. Never "let me know if you need anything".

At the car, not in front of the vendor: "Anyone else in the office listing at the moment? I've got a bit of room before spring hits."

Say one specific thing about the house — the light in the back room, the ceiling height, how the block sits. Agents deal with tradies who grunt and leave. Being the one who noticed is most of why they call back.

If the vendor's there, say goodbye to them properly. They're a seller now and a buyer in six weeks, and they talk to their next agent about who took the photos.

After you deliver

24 hours after: "How'd the home open go? What's next — happy to hold you a slot this week."

Ask about the listing, not the photos. "Did you like them?" invites a critique; "how'd it go?" invites a conversation.

The moment they say the photos are good: "Glad they worked. Would you mind leaving us a Google review? Takes ten seconds, I'll text you the link."

Ask while they're happiest. That window is about an hour wide and then it's gone.

Studio — what's free and what isn't

Photos I shot → decluttering included. Costs nothing at the margin, makes the work better than whoever they used before, and it's a real reason to book you over a cheaper shooter.

Photos I didn't shoot → that's Studio, and it's paid. Their old listings, phone photos, rentals, stock that's been sitting there.

"Anything I shoot, decluttering's included. If you want to run your own photos or old listings through it, that's the Studio side — five bucks an image, same day."

Never name Studio on a cold call — it turns a favour into a demo. Name it in the follow-up email. The exception: if they ask "how'd you do that?", say it straight away — that's them pulling.

Never give Studio away free on every shoot forever. Free-per-shoot trains them to never pay, and then there's no product. One trial of credits per account, then it's a price.

On every delivery email: "Want any of these staged or decluttered? Send the file numbers back and I'll turn them around today — $5 an image to declutter, $25 to stage."

Drop the images INTO the email, don't link them. He's reading this on a phone between appointments — he will not tap through to a gallery to look at two photos. Embedded, he sees the work without deciding to. That's the whole point of the email.

Before/after pairs, not just the after. Without the before there's nothing to notice. Label them plainly — "before / after" — and add no commentary. The pictures make the point; saying it out loud turns it into a dig at his listing.

Free demo video — delivering it to a new agent

Only for an agent you've never worked with, and only for one worth the shoot slot — a top lister, or the way into an office. One per agency, once. Write that rule down for yourself, not for them.

A video costs you a slot plus the edit. Cleaned photos cost you twenty minutes. Photos are the cheap door-opener — video is the thing you sell once the door's open. If you're spending a video, it has to come back with a booking attached.

Send it fast, while they still remember agreeing to it. Show, price, ask — same three beats as the phone script.

The price line is what stops it being a gift. Without a number it reads as a freebie and they file it. With a number it reads as a sample and they price the next one.

Never ask if they liked it. "Hope you like it" and "let me know what you think" both invite a compliment instead of a booking. End on the job, not the work.

A top agent who likes you is the way into everyone they sit with. That's the actual prize — bigger than their own listings. But it only works after money has changed hands once, or you're still the free video guy.

The follow-up cadence — after any free or discounted job

The demo is the ticket. The follow-up is the play. Free work that isn't followed up is just free work. This is where every account has been leaking.

Same day — deliver it, with the price on it and one question.

That one's on me — wanted you to see what we do. On its own it's $[X], or $[Y] with the photo package.

What have you got coming up?

Day 3–4 — one nudge, hung on a live listing of theirs.

Saw you've got [address] on — want me to do that one?

Two weeks — same shape, different property.

Are you shooting [address] this week? Happy to keep a slot.

Then monthly. Light, always about a property, never about the past. Log the date in Calls so it actually happens.

The one rule. Every follow-up hangs on one of THEIR listings — never on your favour.

"Saw you've got 7 Trigger Loop on — want me to do that one?" — asking for work.

"Just following up on those photos I sent."

Asking for a favour. Reminds them they owe you, and people avoid anyone they owe. Never send "just checking in" — it has no content and everyone knows what it means.

Don't reopen a finished conversation. If they've already given feedback and you've already fixed it, that thread is done. The next call is about a property, not about the photos.

Save free shoots for top agents. A shoot costs a whole slot; two cleaned photos cost twenty minutes and buy the same ticket. Use the slot on someone listing 40+ a year — use the photos on everyone else.

When they're locked in with someone: don't ask them to switch. Ask to be the backup.

Any chance I could be your backup for when your usual guy's booked out?

No switching, no disloyalty, no decision — and their photographer will be unavailable eventually. Backup is how you get the overflow job, and the overflow job is where the comparison happens.

Stop when they say no. Following up is not the same as not hearing them. A clear no, or three unanswered touches, means park them for a month and move on.
