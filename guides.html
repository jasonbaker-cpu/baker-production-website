Listing Media Guides

Plain-English answers to the questions that come up before, during and after a Perth listing shoot — drone rules, staging disclosure, floor plans and light.

These guides cover the four listing-media questions Perth agents and sellers ask us most: whether you can fly your own drone over a listing in WA, what you have to disclose when a photo is virtually staged, whether a listing needs a 2D or a 3D floor plan, and what time of day a house should be photographed. Each one gives the short answer first, then the detail, and links the authority it relies on — CASA for drones, the Australian Consumer Law, the ACCC and Consumer Protection WA for advertising. They are general information, not legal advice.

01

Drone rules for real estate in WA

Flying a drone to photograph a property for sale or lease is commercial use under CASA rules, even with a small drone. The guide covers operator accreditation and registration, the standard operating conditions (daylight, line of sight, below 120 metres, 30 metres from people), the airport no-fly zones around Perth Airport, Jandakot and RAAF Base Pearce, neighbours and privacy, and what to ask a drone operator before you book.

Virtual staging disclosure

Virtual staging is legal in WA, but a staged or edited photo is still a representation about the property. The guide explains how the Australian Consumer Law and the WA agents’ Code of Conduct apply, why the ACCC says fine print can’t contradict the main message, the difference between staging a room and changing the property, and practical labelling rules.

Where each guide fits in a listing

Most of these questions come up at a predictable point in a campaign, so it helps to read them in order.

Before you book. Decide what the listing needs. If the block, the outlook or the distance to the beach is part of the story, you want aerials — and if you were thinking of flying them yourself, the drone rules guide sets out what CASA requires and where Perth’s airport zones rule a flight out. Then pick a time: the sun planner shows when the front of the house is lit, which matters most for west- and south-facing homes. A twilight shot is booked around sunset, which moves by about two hours between winter and summer in Perth.

On shoot day. The house only gets one shoot, so preparation counts. The best-time guide ends with a prep checklist. If the property is tenanted, the rental photography page explains the notice and entry rules that apply in WA and the tenant’s say over advertising photos, with links to Consumer Protection WA.

After the shoot. Two decisions follow the photos. If an empty room is going to be staged, or a photo edited beyond normal colour and exposure correction, read the disclosure guide before it goes on a portal. And when the floor plan is ordered, the 2D vs 3D guide helps you decide whether a 3D plan or a site plan is worth adding.

How these guides are written

Each guide starts from the primary source rather than another photographer’s blog. Drone rules come from the Civil Aviation Safety Authority, which regulates drone flying in WA. Advertising and disclosure come from the Australian Consumer Law, ACCC guidance, the WA Code of Conduct for agents and Consumer Protection WA. Sources are listed at the bottom of each guide so you can check them yourself.

Where a guide gives a Baker price, it matches our pricing page. Where we could not find a rule — for example, published REIWA guidance specifically on virtual staging — the guide says so rather than guessing. The regulatory guides carry a “current as at” date; rules change, so for a specific campaign check the source, your licensee or REIWA.
